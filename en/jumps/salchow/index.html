JumpsTelling the Jumps Apart

The difference is in the takeoff.

All six jumps land the same way, on the right foot, facing backward. They differ mainly in how they take off.

Scroll

5 ways to tell them apart

Q1 — Direction1

A takeoff facing forward is an Axel.

The other five jumps take off while skating backward. Only the Axel takes off facing forward.

All six jumps land facing backward. The Axel takes off facing forward, so it has half a rotation more than the others. A double Axel is 2.5 rotations.

Q2 — Toe pick2

Does it take off from the toe pick or the edge?

In a toe jump (toe loop, flip and Lutz), the skater reaches one leg back and plants its toe pick, the teeth at the front of the blade, in the ice to jump. In an edge jump (Salchow and loop, plus the Axel, which takes off facing forward), the skater takes off from the edge without the toe pick.

Q3 — Which toe3

Planting the left toe pick means a toe loop.

In a toe loop, the skater glides in on the right foot and plants the left toe pick to take off. In a flip and a Lutz, the skater glides in on the left foot and plants the right toe pick. The toe pick is on the opposite foot.

A toe loop lands on the same right foot the skater glides in on.

Q4 — Edge4

The edge decides: inside for a flip, outside for a Lutz.

The two jumps that plant the right toe pick differ in which way the edge of the left foot, the takeoff foot, tilts. A flip takes off from the inside edge (the big-toe side), and a Lutz from the outside edge (the little-toe side). If the edge of a Lutz tilts to the inside, it is a takeoff error (an edge error), and the base value goes down.

Q4 — Continued

Only the Lutz rotates against its entry curve.

A Lutz takes off from the outside edge, so it rotates in the direction opposite to the entry curve, the curve it skated in on.

Q5 — Free leg5

A swinging right leg means a Salchow, crossed legs mean a loop.

The two edge jumps differ in how the legs are used. In a Salchow, the skater takes off from the left foot and swings the right leg up. In a loop, the skater jumps off the right foot alone, from a position with the feet crossed.

Names

A jump’s name is its rotations plus its kind.

On a broadcast, a jump is named by its number of rotations followed by its kind, as in “triple Lutz” or “quad toe loop”. Two, three and four rotations are called double, triple and quad.

The Axel has half a rotation more, so a double Axel is 2.5 rotations and a triple Axel is 3.5.

  1. 3 rotations+Lutz= triple Lutz
  2. 4 rotations+Toe loop= quad toe loop
  3. double+Axel= 2.5 rotations

    Reading a Score Sheet

    Axel

    The only one of the six jumps that takes off facing forward. The skater swings the right leg up and through to jump, and lands facing backward, so the jump has half a rotation more than the others. All six jumps end the same way: a landing on the right foot, facing backward.

    Step forward, then jump facing ahead.

    The skater glides backward, steps forward onto the left foot, and swings the right leg through to jump. The Axel is the only jump that takes off facing forward, and because the skater can see what lies ahead, it is said to feel frightening in a different way from the other jumps.

    Short Program or Free Skate, an Axel is required.

    In the Short Program, every skater must jump either a double Axel (2.5 rotations) or a triple Axel (3.5 rotations). In the Free Skate, too, one of the jumps has to be an Axel.

    Lutz

    The skater glides backward on the outside edge of the left foot and jumps by planting the right toe pick (the teeth at the front of the blade) in the ice. The jump rotates the opposite way to the curve of the approach.

    A long glide backward leads into the jump.

    This jump often follows a long, diagonal glide backward toward a corner of the rink. In recent years, though, some skaters jump straight out of steps, so it has become harder to tell the jump by its approach alone.

    The left foot’s edge is the outside edge.

    The skater keeps the edge of the left takeoff foot tilted to the outside (the little-toe side) and jumps by planting the right toe pick. Because the jump turns against the curve on an outside edge, the speed of the glide cannot be put into the rotation. It is said to be the hardest jump after the Axel. The tilt in the diagram is one measured example.

    Compare

    Its edge is the opposite of the flip’s.

    Both jumps glide on the left foot and take off by planting the right toe pick. The flip takes off from the inside edge; the Lutz, from the outside edge.

    Rotation runs against the curve.

    Seen from diagonally above, the curve of the glide and the rotation of the body in the air go in opposite directions.

    An inward tilt of the edge lowers the score.

    If the edge tilts inward at takeoff (informally, a “flutz”), an “e” is added on the score sheet and the base value goes down. If the edge is unclear, a “!” is added instead. The officials are allowed to check the tilt of the edge in slow-motion replay.

    Flip

    A jump that takes off backward. The skater rides the inside edge of the left foot and jumps by planting the right toe pick (the teeth at the front of the blade) in the ice.

    Turn around, then jump.

    The skater glides forward, turns to face backward just before the jump (for example with a three-turn, a turn made on one foot), and plants the right toe pick straight away. The telltale sign is that the skater seems to look back over the shoulder while jumping.

    The left foot’s edge is the inside edge.

    The edge of the left takeoff foot is the inside edge (the big-toe side). The skater glides almost straight into the takeoff and does not tilt the edge deeply. The difficulty is timing the toe-pick plant while holding that unstable inside edge. The tilt in the diagram is one measured example.

    Its edge is the opposite of the Lutz’s.

    Both jumps glide on the left foot and take off by planting the right toe pick. The flip takes off from the inside edge; the Lutz, from the outside edge.

    An outward tilt of the edge lowers the score.

    As with the Lutz, the tilt of the edge at takeoff is judged. If it tilts outward, an “e” or a “!” is added.

    Toe loop

    The skater glides backward on the right foot and jumps by planting the toe pick (the teeth at the front of the blade) of the left foot in the ice. The landing is on the right foot as well.

    It takes off from the left toe pick.

    The skater plants the left toe pick in the ice and uses that push to jump. Because the toe pick helps, it is considered the easiest of the six jumps.

    Often the second jump of a combination.

    It is the jump most often used as the second jump of a jump combination, in which two jumps are done in a row. It is often performed as a quad, too. If a skater plants a toe pick for the second jump, it is most likely a toe loop.

    See “Program & Scoring” for details

    Taking off facing forward means a downgrade.

    The toe loop is considered a jump whose takeoff is easy to cheat. If the skater clearly takes off facing forward, it is treated as a jump well short of full rotation (a downgrade, marked “<<” on the score sheet). This call is made on normal-speed replay.

    Salchow

    A jump that takes off backward from the left foot. The toe pick (the teeth at the front of the blade) is not used; the skater swings the right leg up to jump.

    From a forward turn, onto the left inside edge.

    The skater glides forward, turns to face backward with a turn made on one foot (a three-turn), and settles onto the inside edge of the left foot.

    The right leg swings up and forward.

    The right leg, stretched out behind, swings forward and up while the arms are pulled in, and the skater jumps. The timing of the swing is the key. Before takeoff, the legs are said to look like an inverted V, though some skaters in recent years do not fit that description.

    Often the last jump of a three-jump combination.

    In a combination of three jumps, it is often the third jump, coming after a small jump in between (an Euler).

    Loop

    The skater glides backward and jumps from the right foot alone, from a posture in which the legs look crossed. The toe pick (the teeth at the front of the blade) is not used.

    The jump starts from a pose like sitting down.

    With both feet on the ice, one in front of the other, the skater glides along a large curve, settles into a posture like sitting down, and then jumps.

    Takeoff is from the right foot alone.

    The legs stay crossed, and the skater takes off from the right foot alone.

    Also used as the second jump of a combination.

    After the toe loop, it is the jump most often used as the second jump of a jump combination. The skater takes off from the same right foot that just landed, without using the toe pick, so it is harder than the toe loop.
